How did architecture
transform over
500 years?

The transformation of architecture is not a story of individual genius. It is a story of shifting relations — between architects, between building types, between patrons and cities and eras.

We scraped 211 buildings by 8 Italian architects from Wikipedia, audited the data, and asked: how do the contributions of architects reflect — or drive — the structural transformation of architecture across time?

01 — Research Question

The transformation of
architectural program
and the architects who shaped it

Did architects drive the transformation of architecture — or did the transformation of the world produce different kinds of architects? This visualization sits with that question rather than resolving it.

We trace the relationship between individual architectural contributions and the structural shift in building typology across five centuries: from the dominance of the religious to the rise of the cultural, from local concentration to global reach, from singular patrons to plural publics.

The dataset — 8 Italian architects, 208 buildings, scraped from Wikipedia — is a partial and biased record. We make that partiality visible, because the gaps in the data are also part of the story.

02 — Audience & Design Intent

This visualization addresses designers, architects, and academic researchers — audiences comfortable with abstraction, incomplete records, and non-linear reading. We do not simplify. We use monochrome tone to encode time, dot clusters to encode program, and particle flow to encode influence. The visual language is analytical and restrained; the narrative is open.

03 — Visualization I

Typological kinship
across time

Each node is an architect. The cluster of dots within encodes their building program — larger dots signal dominant types. Lines connect architects who share significant typological overlap: a structural kinship that crosses centuries. Darker nodes are older; lighter, newer. Time is encoded as tone — from shadow to light, 1508 to 1937.

Notice how Borromini and Maderno are tightly bound through religious typology, while Piano and Nervi share a more varied, commercial-cultural connection. The network reveals not just who built what — but who built like whom.

04 — Visualization II

Every building,
every gap

Each dot is one building. Eight architects, eight column groups, eight building types per group. Dot size encodes the weight of that type within the architect's program — so Vittone's single enormous religious cluster contrasts sharply with Piano's sprawling cultural spread.

Opacity encodes data completeness. Faded dots are buildings where fields were missing in Wikipedia — country, coordinates, image, or type. The gaps are not errors. They are the shape of collective memory: what gets documented, what gets forgotten, and what gets lost entirely.

05 — Visualization III

Where they built
— and how far

Each node marks an architect's birthplace. Dots mark individual buildings. Particles flow from birthplace to building — the arc of influence made spatial. The contrast is immediate: Vittone's particles barely leave Piedmont; Piano's reach Sydney, Singapore, New Caledonia.

Geographic reach is itself a transformation. The pre-modern architect built within a city, a region, a court. The modern architect builds for a globalized cultural economy. Scroll to zoom. Click a name to isolate their geography.

06 — Findings & Reflection

What the data reveals
— and what it refuses to

Our research question asked how architects contributed to — or reflected — the structural transformation of architectural program. Three findings stand out. First, the transformation is real and measurable: religious dominance collapses after 1775, cultural typology explodes in the 20th century. Second, geographic reach expands in direct proportion to this shift — the architects of cultural institutions are also the architects of global practice. Third, and most unsettling: the data itself is shaped by a bias that mirrors the transformation. Contemporary, internationally visible work is better documented; older, locally embedded work is systematically underrepresented.

Finding 01 — The programmatic shift

Religious typology dominates until the mid-18th century. After Rossi, cultural and residential programs begin to compete. With Nervi and Piano, the museum and the public building become the primary vehicles of architectural ambition — mirroring the secularization of collective meaning.

Finding 02 — Vittone's singularity

100% of Vittone's recorded buildings are religious. All in Piedmont. No other architect in this dataset approaches this degree of typological and geographic concentration. His work is not a node in a network — it is a closed system. An architecture without successors in the data.

Finding 03 — The visibility asymmetry

Renzo Piano has 55 buildings in our dataset; Borromini has 13. This is not a measure of importance — it is a measure of Wikipedia's attention. 76% of records have no image. Older, locally embedded, less-touristed work is systematically underdocumented. The data has a geography of attention.

Finding 04 — Structural kinship vs. influence

The network reveals typological kinship, not direct influence. Borromini and Maderno share religious program not because one followed the other, but because both operated in the same structural conditions. The transformation of architecture may be less about individual contribution than about the shifting structure of patronage, city, and era.
